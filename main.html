1. Introduction and Personas

1.1 Context

Pollinator-dependent crops account for a substantial share of agricultural output across Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands. Climatic conditions during crop flowering directly govern bee foraging activity and, by extension, fruit and seed set. Farmers and advisors rarely have access to spatially explicit, crop-specific assessments of how often, and where, weather conditions are suitable for pollination. This project develops a regional Pollination Suitability Index (PSI) computed from daily observed climate data and cross-referenced with crop distribution, providing decision-relevant maps and statistics for three key pollination-dependent crops: rapeseed (Brassica napus), temperate fruit (TEMF), and sunflower (Helianthus annuus).

2. Research Questions

Three research questions structure the analysis and link directly to persona needs.

  • Q1. Where is spring climate suitability for pollination highest and most stable across DE-BE-NL during crop-specific bloom windows?
  • Q2. Where does each crop’s geographic distribution overlap with unsuitable or variable climate conditions, and which regions carry the greatest crop-weighted deficit exposure?
  • Q3. Are crop-weighted suitability and deficit exposure patterns spatially clustered at the NUTS-2 level, and if so, where are the significant hotspots?

3. Data

3.1 E-OBS Daily Climate Grids (v27.0e)

Climate input is sourced from E-OBS, a daily gridded observational dataset produced by Copernicus Climate Change Service and the EURO4M project. The dataset covers Europe at 0.1 degree regular latitude-longitude resolution (about 7 to 9 km at study latitudes). Two variables are used: ensemble mean daily mean temperature (TG, degC) and ensemble mean daily precipitation sum (RR, mm/day). Temporal coverage spans 2011 to 2022 (12 years), enabling interannual variability assessment of seasonal pollination suitability. Layers are clipped to the union of NUTS-2 boundaries for DE, BE, and NL before analysis to reduce processing overhead.

3.2 SPAM 2020 - Crop Harvested Area

Crop distribution is represented using SPAM 2020 version 2r0 (IFPRI). SPAM allocates national agricultural statistics to a 5 arc-minute grid using land use, suitability, and irrigation inputs. The variable used is harvested area in hectares per grid cell (TA: all technologies combined). Crops included are rapeseed (RAPE), temperate fruit (TEMF), and sunflower (SUNF). Each raster is reprojected to the E-OBS 0.1 degree grid using bilinear resampling, then normalized by grid cell area (ha) to obtain crop fraction (0 to 1). Values are clamped to [0, 1] to remove reprojection artifacts.

3.3 NUTS 2024 Administrative Boundaries

Administrative boundaries follow the NUTS 2024 classification from Eurostat (GeoPackage, EPSG:3035; https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/nuts). NUTS is a hierarchical regional system:

  • NUTS 0 = country level (DE, BE, NL)
  • NUTS 1 = major socio-economic regions
  • NUTS 2 = basic regions for regional policies
  • NUTS 3 = smaller regions for detailed diagnostics

This analysis uses NUTS level 2. The study area includes 61 NUTS-2 regions: 38 in Germany, 12 in the Netherlands, and 11 in Belgium. Boundaries are transformed to EPSG:4326 for raster operations and EPSG:3035 for spatial weights and LISA analysis.

4. Methods

4.1 Pollination Suitability Index (PSI)

A daily PSI is computed for each E-OBS grid cell and day using piecewise linear scoring for temperature and precipitation. The temperature score increases linearly from 0 at 10 degC to 1 at 19 degC, remains 1 from 19 to 30 degC, then decreases linearly to 0 at 40 degC. The precipitation score is 1 for <= 1 mm/day, decreases linearly to 0 at 5 mm/day, and is 0 above 5 mm/day. Daily PSI is the product of both scores, constrained to [0, 1]:

PSI(s, t) = temp_score(TG(s, t)) * rain_score(RR(s, t))

The temperature parameterization used in code is:

  • t_zero_low = 10
  • t_opt_low = 19
  • t_opt_high = 30
  • t_zero_high = 40

This aligns with a general bee activity envelope in which flight is feasible roughly between 10 and 40 degC, with highest foraging efficiency commonly reported around 20 to 30 degC. Weather sensitivity of bee activity and production outcomes is also consistent with recent synthesis work on short-term weather impacts on honey production (Vincze et al., 2025).

This continuous formulation captures partial suitability and avoids hard binary cutoffs. Additional variables such as wind speed and radiation were considered but not included to keep the suitability index interpretable and focused on the dominant climatic controls.

4.2 Crop-Specific Bloom Windows

PSI is computed for the full 2011 to 2022 period, then subset by crop bloom windows:

  • Rapeseed (RAPE): 01 Apr to 31 May
  • Temperate fruit (TEMF): 05 Apr to 10 May
  • Sunflower (SUNF): 10 Jul to 10 Aug

This avoids misrepresenting sunflower by forcing it into a spring-only baseline.

4.3 Temporal Summaries

Within each crop window and across all years, four pixel-level summaries are computed:

  • Mean PSI: average daily suitability across all days and years in the window.
  • Equivalent suitable days per year: summed daily PSI across all window-years divided by number of years.
  • Deficit days per year: equivalent count for 1 - PSI.
  • Interannual SD: standard deviation of annual mean PSI across the 12 years.

4.4 Crop Fraction and Weighted Metrics

Two composite indicators combine climate suitability with crop distribution:

  • Crop-weighted suitability: psi_mean_crop * crop_frac.
  • Crop-weighted deficit exposure: deficit_days_per_year_crop * crop_frac.

The first indicates overlap of favorable climate and crop presence. The second captures unsuitable climate burden weighted by crop fraction.

4.5 Regional Aggregation and Spatial Autocorrelation

Raster indicators are aggregated to NUTS-2 regions using area-weighted zonal means (exactextractr). Spatial autocorrelation is assessed with Global Moran’s I and Local Moran’s I (LISA) using queen contiguity and row-standardized weights. Significance is tested with 999 Monte Carlo permutations and alpha <= 0.05.

library(tibble)

select_window_by_mmdd <- function(dates, start_mmdd, end_mmdd) {
  years_chr <- format(dates, "%Y")
  start_dates <- as.Date(paste0(years_chr, "-", start_mmdd))
  end_dates <- as.Date(paste0(years_chr, "-", end_mmdd))
  as.logical(!is.na(start_dates) & !is.na(end_dates) & dates >= start_dates & dates <= end_dates)
}

sum_or_na <- function(x) {
  if (all(is.na(x))) return(NA_real_)
  sum(x, na.rm = TRUE)
}

summarize_window <- function(psi_daily_full, dates_full, start_mmdd, end_mmdd) {
  sel <- select_window_by_mmdd(dates_full, start_mmdd, end_mmdd)
  if (!any(sel)) {
    stop("No layers selected for window ", start_mmdd, " to ", end_mmdd)
  }

  psi_w <- psi_daily_full[[which(sel)]]
  dates_w <- dates_full[sel]
  years_w <- unique(format(dates_w, "%Y"))

  mean_r <- app(psi_w, mean, na.rm = TRUE)
  equiv_days_r <- app(psi_w, sum_or_na)
  deficit_days_r <- app(1 - psi_w, sum_or_na)
  equiv_days_per_year_r <- equiv_days_r / length(years_w)
  deficit_days_per_year_r <- deficit_days_r / length(years_w)

  yearly_means <- list()
  for (yy in years_w) {
    idx_y <- which(format(dates_w, "%Y") == yy)
    yearly_means[[yy]] <- app(psi_w[[idx_y]], mean, na.rm = TRUE)
  }
  interannual_sd_r <- app(terra::rast(yearly_means), sd, na.rm = TRUE)

  list(
    mean = mean_r,
    equiv_days = equiv_days_r,
    equiv_days_per_year = equiv_days_per_year_r,
    deficit_days = deficit_days_r,
    deficit_days_per_year = deficit_days_per_year_r,
    interannual_sd = interannual_sd_r,
    dates = dates_w,
    n_days = length(dates_w),
    n_years = length(years_w)
  )
}

make_crop_fraction <- function(spam_raster, template_raster) {
  spam_aligned <- project(spam_raster, template_raster, method = "bilinear")
  cell_area_m2 <- tryCatch(
    cellSize(template_raster, unit = "m"),
    error = function(e) cellSize(template_raster)
  )
  cell_area_ha <- cell_area_m2 / 10000
  clamp(spam_aligned / cell_area_ha, 0, 1)
}

build_region_day_table <- function(psi_stack, dates_stack, admin_sf) {
  admin_ts <- sf::st_transform(admin_sf, terra::crs(psi_stack))
  admin_labels <- sf::st_drop_geometry(admin_ts) %>%
    mutate(region_id = as.character(NUTS_ID))

  label_candidates <- c("NAME_LATN", "NUTS_NAME", "NAME_ENGL")
  label_col <- label_candidates[label_candidates %in% names(admin_labels)]
  admin_labels$region_label <- if (length(label_col) > 0) as.character(admin_labels[[label_col[1]]]) else admin_labels$region_id

  daily_wide <- exactextractr::exact_extract(psi_stack, admin_ts, "mean") %>% tibble::as_tibble()
  if (nrow(daily_wide) != nrow(admin_labels)) {
    stop("Row mismatch while extracting daily regional PSI.")
  }
  daily_wide$region_id <- admin_labels$region_id

  layer_lookup <- tibble(
    layer = paste0("mean.", names(psi_stack)),
    layer_index = seq_len(terra::nlyr(psi_stack)),
    date = as.Date(dates_stack)
  )

  daily_wide %>%
    tidyr::pivot_longer(-region_id, names_to = "layer", values_to = "psi_daily_mean") %>%
    left_join(layer_lookup, by = "layer") %>%
    left_join(admin_labels %>% select(region_id, region_label), by = "region_id")
}

compute_daily_psi <- function(tg_stack, rr_stack,
                              t_zero_low = 10, t_opt_low = 19,
                              t_opt_high = 30, t_zero_high = 40,
                              rr_opt = 1, rr_zero = 5) {
  temp_score <- ifel(
    is.na(tg_stack), NA,
    ifel(
      tg_stack <= t_zero_low | tg_stack >= t_zero_high, 0,
      ifel(
        tg_stack >= t_opt_low & tg_stack <= t_opt_high, 1,
        ifel(
          tg_stack < t_opt_low,
          (tg_stack - t_zero_low) / (t_opt_low - t_zero_low),
          (t_zero_high - tg_stack) / (t_zero_high - t_opt_high)
        )
      )
    )
  )

  rain_score <- ifel(
    is.na(rr_stack), NA,
    ifel(rr_stack <= rr_opt, 1, ifel(rr_stack >= rr_zero, 0, (rr_zero - rr_stack) / (rr_zero - rr_opt)))
  )
  clamp(temp_score * rain_score, lower = 0, upper = 1)
}

run_global_moran <- function(sf_obj, value_col, nsim = 999) {
  sf_ok <- sf_obj %>%
    mutate(value = .data[[value_col]]) %>%
    filter(is.finite(value))
  if (nrow(sf_ok) < 3) return(tibble(moran_i = NA_real_, p_value = NA_real_, n_regions = nrow(sf_ok)))

  nb_ok <- st_contiguity(sf_ok)
  keep <- lengths(nb_ok) > 0
  sf_ok <- sf_ok[keep, ]
  if (nrow(sf_ok) < 3) return(tibble(moran_i = NA_real_, p_value = NA_real_, n_regions = nrow(sf_ok)))
  nb_ok <- st_contiguity(sf_ok)
  lw_ok <- st_weights(nb_ok, style = "W")

  gm <- global_moran(sf_ok$value, nb_ok, lw_ok)
  gp <- global_moran_perm(sf_ok$value, nb_ok, lw_ok, nsim = nsim)
  tibble(moran_i = as.numeric(gm$I), p_value = as.numeric(gp$p.value), n_regions = nrow(sf_ok))
}

    out$crop <- cr
    out$metric <- mt
    out
  }))

    filter(is.finite(value))
  nb_ok <- st_contiguity(sf_ok)
  keep <- lengths(nb_ok) > 0
  sf_ok <- sf_ok[keep, ]
  if (nrow(sf_ok) < 3) {
    next
  }
  nb_ok <- st_contiguity(sf_ok)
  lw_ok <- st_weights(nb_ok, style = "W")
  lisa <- local_moran(sf_ok$value, nb_ok, lw_ok)
  lisa_tbl <- as_tibble(lisa)

  if ("cluster" %in% names(lisa_tbl)) {
    cl <- as.character(lisa_tbl$cluster)
  } else if ("mean" %in% names(lisa_tbl)) {
    cl <- as.character(lisa_tbl$mean)
  } else {
    # Fallback cluster labels when sfdep doesn't expose cluster classes:
    # classify quadrants using standardized value and standardized spatial lag.
    z <- as.numeric(scale(sf_ok$value))
    lw_spdep <- spdep::nb2listw(nb_ok, style = "W", zero.policy = TRUE)
    lag_z <- as.numeric(scale(spdep::lag.listw(lw_spdep, z, zero.policy = TRUE)))
    cl <- dplyr::case_when(
      z >= 0 & lag_z >= 0 ~ "High-High",
      z >= 0 & lag_z <  0 ~ "High-Low",
      z <  0 & lag_z >= 0 ~ "Low-High",
      z <  0 & lag_z <  0 ~ "Low-Low",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    )
  }

  sf_ok <- sf_ok %>%
    st_drop_geometry() %>%
    transmute(
      region_id,
      tmp_i = lisa_tbl$ii,
      tmp_p = lisa_tbl$p_ii,
      tmp_cl = as.character(cl),
      tmp_sig = ifelse(
        is.na(tmp_p),
        NA_character_,
        ifelse(tmp_p <= 0.05, tmp_cl, "Not significant")
      )
    )

  admin_stats_3035 <- admin_stats_3035 %>%
    left_join(sf_ok, by = "region_id") %>%
    rename(
      !!paste0("lisa_i_crop_bad_", cr) := tmp_i,
      !!paste0("lisa_p_crop_bad_", cr) := tmp_p,
      !!paste0("lisa_cl_crop_bad_", cr) := tmp_cl,
      !!paste0("lisa_sig_crop_bad_", cr) := tmp_sig
    )

5. Results

5.1 Baseline Climate Suitability (Q1)

The mean PSI map for the Mar-Jun baseline window reveals a clear spatial gradient across the study region. Eastern and north-eastern Germany (Brandenburg, Sachsen, Mecklenburg-Vorpommern) shows the highest mean PSI values, indicating that temperature and rainfall conditions are most frequently within the suitable range during the spring bloom period. The coastal north (Schleswig-Holstein, NL north) and the Alpine south (Bayern, Baden-Wurttemberg high elevations) show reduced suitability, the former driven by excess precipitation and cool temperatures, the latter by late-season cold. The equivalent suitable days map mirrors this pattern: eastern German regions average approximately 20-25 suitable days per Mar-Jun season, while Belgium and the Netherlands average 10-15. For Lars, this baseline immediately stratifies the study region into a higher-suitability eastern tier and a lower-suitability western tier, informing regional premium structure. For Anneke, it identifies the eastern regions as potential benchmarks for best-practice pollination conditions.

The interannual SD map shows that instability is moderate (0.06-0.14) and spatially diffuse, with slightly elevated variability in central Germany. The limited spread across 12 years constrains the confidence of this estimate, but the pattern suggests that the eastern high-suitability zone is not only better on average but also somewhat more consistent, which is a secondary advantage for both personas.

5.2 Rapeseed - Crop Distribution and Exposure (Q2)

Rapeseed is the most spatially concentrated crop in the SPAM data, with the highest harvested fractions in north-eastern Germany (Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Brandenburg, Sachsen-Anhalt) reaching up to 10-12% of cell area. Belgium shows scattered smaller concentrations. The Netherlands has minimal rapeseed presence. The crop-weighted suitability map confirms that north-eastern Germany holds the best combination of high PSI and high crop intensity, and these are Anneke’s primary demonstration-plot candidate regions. The crop-weighted deficit exposure map tells a more sobering story for Lars: despite relatively good average PSI, the north-east also accumulates the highest absolute deficit exposure simply because it has so much crop area. Mecklenburg-Vorpommern carries the highest regional deficit burden, with multiple adjacent regions also elevated, which the LISA analysis confirms as a statistically significant High-High cluster (p < 0.05, Moran’s I = 0.577). The Netherlands shows a Low-Low cluster, reflecting both low rapeseed presence and moderate climate conditions. This asymmetry, where the best suitability and the highest risk co-occur in the same zone due to crop concentration, is a key finding for crop insurance pricing.

5.3 Temperate Fruit - Crop Distribution and Exposure (Q2)

Temperate fruit cultivation is geographically distinct from rapeseed: the highest TEMF fractions appear in the Rhine-Maas corridor straddling the NL/BE border, and in southern Germany (Bodensee region, Rhine valley). Eastern Germany, which dominates the rapeseed picture, is largely absent from TEMF maps. The PSI window for TEMF (5 Apr - 10 May) is slightly narrower and earlier, producing somewhat different suitability patterns: southern Germany, though appearing suboptimal in the rapeseed window, shows adequate PSI for the shorter fruit-bloom window. The deficit exposure map highlights the NL/BE Rhine-Maas cluster and the Bodensee area as the two primary risk zones for TEMF, which is useful for Anneke because her cooperative spans this exact cross-border area. The LISA map shows a localized High-High cluster at the NL/BE border and an isolated High-High outlier in southern Bavaria, with the rest of the study area not significant. Moran’s I is moderate (0.295, p = 0.01), suggesting weaker overall clustering for TEMF than for rapeseed and reflecting TEMF’s more fragmented spatial distribution.

5.4 Sunflower - Crop Distribution and Exposure (Q2)

Sunflower has a markedly different spatial and temporal profile. The SPAM data shows very low sunflower fractions across most of the study region; only a few cells in eastern Germany and isolated areas near the Belgian/French border register non-trivial values. The sunflower bloom window (10 Jul - 10 Aug) falls in mid-summer when temperatures across the study region are systematically higher, yielding high PSI values almost everywhere, and equivalent suitable days typically exceed 20 per season. As a result, crop-weighted deficit exposure values are near-zero for most regions. The LISA map shows only a small High-High cluster in eastern Germany (Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Brandenburg) driven by the combination of above-average crop presence and the slightly drier, hotter continental conditions that occasionally push temperatures toward the upper suitability limit. For Lars, sunflower carries low systematic climate risk across the study area. For Anneke, the more relevant concern is local hive density and crop-pollinator synchronization, which falls outside the scope of this analysis.

5.5 Spatial Clustering Summary (Q3)

Across all three crops, crop-weighted suitability and deficit exposure show statistically significant positive spatial autocorrelation (all Global Moran’s I > 0.29, p <= 0.01). This confirms that climate-driven pollination risk is not randomly distributed but structured, and adjacent regions tend to share similar exposure levels. For Anneke this means that regional advisory campaigns are more efficient than farm-by-farm outreach: targeting a High-High cluster reaches multiple high-risk farms simultaneously. For Lars, it means that regional portfolio diversification does not fully hedge climate risk because holding policies in multiple regions within the same cluster does not reduce correlated seasonal exposure. Rapeseed shows the strongest clustering (I = 0.577), making it the crop where spatial targeting delivers the greatest analytical leverage.

6. Conclusions

Spring pollination climate across DE-BE-NL is spatially structured and crop-dependent. Eastern Germany consistently offers the most suitable temperature and rainfall conditions during spring bloom windows, while the coastal north and Alpine south face greater climatic constraints. However, because rapeseed cultivation is also concentrated in the east, the same regions that benefit climatically also accumulate the highest absolute crop-weighted deficit exposure, which has direct implications for risk assessment and advisory prioritization. Temperate fruit and sunflower show different geographic profiles, with fruit risk concentrated at the NL/BE border and sunflower carrying low systematic risk across the study area. Spatial autocorrelation is strong and significant for all crops, validating a regional clustering approach over individual farm-level analysis. For Lars Hoffmann, the LISA maps and ranking tables provide the spatial risk stratification needed to differentiate regional premiums. For Anneke Visser, the crop-weighted suitability surfaces identify benchmark zones and priority intervention clusters for advisory deployment.
